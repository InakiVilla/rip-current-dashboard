Rip Current Detection Model Using MobileNetV3-Large with Incrementing Dataset Sizes

How much labeled footage does a lightweight neural network need before it can reliably tell a rip current from ordinary surf? One fixed model, one training setup, and eight nested datasets from 500 to 20,000 images.

Rean G. Coyacot and Vicente Iñaki B. Villa Adviser: Christine F. Peña, MMath BS Computer Science, Department of Computer, Information Sciences and Mathematics, University of San Carlos, 2026

Abstract

ModelMobileNetV3-Large, ImageNet pretrained
TaskBinary classification: rip current or no rip current
DataRipVIS, nested sets of 500 to 20,000 images

Rip currents are a leading cause of surf-zone rescues, yet beachgoers rarely recognize them. This study evaluated how the amount of labeled training data affects a lightweight convolutional neural network for binary rip current classification. MobileNetV3-Large, initialized with ImageNet weights, was fine-tuned on eight nested datasets of 500 to 20,000 frames derived from the RipVIS benchmark, each with a 77:23 rip to non-rip ratio and a 70/15/15 video-level split, under identical training settings. Models were evaluated using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, F2-score and specificity, together with training time and inference speed.

Performance did not follow a pattern of steadily diminishing gains. F2 ranged from 93.21% to 98.70% and peaked at 2,500 images, where the model missed only 1 of 289 rip current images. At 10,000 and 20,000 images, F2 settled near 94.8% while specificity rose to 92.17%, as larger datasets shifted errors from false alarms toward missed rip currents. Inference took about 8 to 9 ms per frame (112 to 127 frames per second) on an NVIDIA RTX 3050, with a 48.5 MB checkpoint.

The results suggest that moderate dataset sizes may be sufficient for lightweight rip current classifiers under the conditions tested, and that adding more frames from the same videos does not guarantee better performance. Because each size was trained once and the test sets drew on a small number of videos, these findings should be confirmed with repeated runs and broader coastal data.

Why dataset size, not a bigger model

Rip currents cause most surf-zone rescues, yet beachgoers rarely recognize them. Deep learning can spot them in coastal imagery, but most published work relies on heavy models and very large annotated datasets.

This study holds the model and training settings fixed and varies only the amount of labeled data. MobileNetV3-Large, initialized with ImageNet weights, is fine-tuned as a binary classifier (Rip Current vs Non-Rip Current) at every dataset size. The aim is to see how performance changes with data and whether additional images return smaller gains.

How performance is measured

  • AccuracyShare of all test images classified correctly.
  • PrecisionOf the images flagged as rip currents, how many really were. Low precision means false alarms.
  • RecallOf the real rip currents, how many the model caught. Low recall means missed hazards.
  • F1-scoreBalance of precision and recall.
  • SpecificityOf the images without a rip current, how many the model correctly cleared. A model that calls everything a rip current scores 0%.
  • F2-scoreWeighs recall more heavily than precision, because missing a rip current is the costlier error. This is the headline metric.

How the datasets were built

Every dataset is nested: each smaller set is fully contained in the next larger one, so a change in performance reflects added data rather than a different random draw.

Dataset pipeline

Eight nested sizes, 77% Rip Current to 23% Non-Rip Current, split 70 / 15 / 15.

  2. COCO-listed frames
  3. Resize and pad
  4. Video-level split

  6. 500 to 20,000
  1. Extract RipVIS's sampled framesOnly the frames listed in each split's COCO annotation file are read from the videos with OpenCV, then resized to 224 × 224 with aspect ratio preserved and black padding.
  2. Label by source videoFrames from videos named RipVIS-NR are Non-Rip Current; all others are Rip Current, the positive class.
  3. Exclude four videosRipVIS-070, 071, 110 and 125 are removed because on-screen subtitles or watermarks could become shortcuts unrelated to rip currents.
  4. Assign each video to one splitFrames from all RipVIS splits are pooled, then each video goes to exactly one of train, validation or test, largest videos first, into the split furthest below its 20,000-image quota (seed 42). No video appears in two splits.
  5. Sample and nestWithin each split, all frames are shuffled at random (seed 42), and each dataset takes the first frames of that order up to its class quota, so every smaller dataset sits inside the larger ones.

Limitations found

Video contribution imbalanceRandom sampling follows clip length, so in the 20,000-image dataset one video supplied 2,126 frames while the median video supplied 45.
Temporal similarity29.36% of neighbouring sampled frames from the same video were consecutive frames, which look almost the same.
Few test videosEach test split draws on about 22 rip-current videos and 4 non-rip videos, so a few clips strongly shape the scores.

Training pipeline

Every dataset size is trained with exactly the same settings, so differences in results come from the amount of data.

  5. Sigmoid, threshold 0.5

Reading these results carefully

Class imbalance sets a high floor

With about 77% rip-current images, a model that always answers "rip current" reaches about 77% accuracy and an F2-score near 94.4%. F2 and recall should be read against that reference, and specificity shows whether the model learned to recognise calm water.

A few videos drive the errors

At 20,000 images, all 54 false alarms came from one non-rip video (RipVIS-NR-002), and 115 of the 137 missed rip currents came from one rip video (RipVIS-142). Shifts between sizes often reflect how those clips are handled rather than general learning.

One run per dataset size

Each point comes from a single training run. Small differences between neighbouring sizes may fall within run-to-run variation, so the overall shape of the curve says more than any single step.

Labels come from the source video

Every frame from a rip-current video is labelled Rip Current, even if no rip is visible in that particular frame, so some training and test labels may be noisy.

Scope

Findings apply to MobileNetV3-Large on RipVIS-derived frames at the tested sizes. They do not identify a universally sufficient dataset size, and further validation is needed before applying them to other beaches or to live monitoring.

These are experimental research findings from a thesis study. They are not beach warnings, hazard ratings, or swimmer-safety advice.

Data: RipVIS: Rip Currents Video Instance Segmentation Benchmark for Beach Monitoring and Safety (Dumitriu et al., CVPR 2025). Model: MobileNetV3-Large (Howard et al., ICCV 2019), implemented in PyTorch and TorchVision.
